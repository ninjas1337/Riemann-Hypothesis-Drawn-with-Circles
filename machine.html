The Prime Gearbox

A virtual machine with three shafts. The primes drive one in jerks, the prime number theorem drives another smoothly, and a controller built from the zeros of the zeta function tries to make up the difference. A differential measures the slip that is left.

How the machine works

1 · Sieve gears

Gears with 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 and 13 teeth turn one tooth per number. A gear blocks the light beam when its mark points up, which happens when the number is divisible by its tooth count. When all marks are away, the beam passes. (Six gears certify primes up to 168; beyond that the machine reads the primes from a table.)

2 · Prime shaft

A ratchet advances one tooth per prime and moves the shaft forward by 2√(p·q), the step to the next touching circle. Its position is the circle chain from the explainer.

3 · Smooth shaft

A cam cut to the curve 2·li(y²) − y − 3 turns continuously: the prime number theorem as a piece of metal.

4 · Controller

Wheels on wheels, one per zeta zero. Wheel k turns at speed γk in log-time ln y, with size 4/|ρk + 1|. Its shadow is the control signal. This is in spirit the machine Alan Turing began to cut in brass in 1939.

5 · Differential

Like the differential in a car axle, it outputs the difference between the prime shaft and the smooth shaft plus controller. The needle shows the slip.

What you can see

With the controller off, the needle shows the raw wobble of the primes: forward after dense clusters, back after big gaps, mostly a little behind. Switch on the offset gear and add oscillators one by one: the slip shrinks. With 138 zeros the controller follows the primes closely. Where it has to follow the sudden jump at a prime with only finitely many wheels, it overshoots by about 9 % (the Gibbs phenomenon).

The Riemann Hypothesis, in machine language: the system that produces the primes has all its poles on the stability boundary. Every zero is an undamped oscillator. In log-time u = ln y, the prime-power impulses Λ(n)·δ(u − ln n) have the Laplace transform Σ Λ(n)·n−s = −ζ′(s)/ζ(s): the zeta zeros are the poles of that transfer function. A pole to the right of the line would be an unstable mode, and the slip would grow without limit.

Move the first pole to the right and the controller grows a mode the real primes do not have: the slip runs away. Up to the range this machine can run, the primes only agree with poles on the line. That is a picture of the hypothesis, not a proof of it.
