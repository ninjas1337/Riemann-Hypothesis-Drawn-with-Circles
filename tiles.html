Growing a tiling from the numbers

Go through the numbers in order and turn each into tiles. A prime can only be a thin strip, 1×p. Every other number brings all of its rectangles with sides of at least 2: 4 gives 2×2, 6 gives 2×3, 8 gives 2×4, 9 gives 3×3, 12 gives both 2×6 and 3×4. Every new tile goes where it keeps the cluster as tight and round as possible. Watch which gaps close, and what the primes do.

Colour = the short side: 1 (a prime's strip) 2 3 4 or 5 6 and up Dark background showing through = a gap.

The rule

Each new tile is tried against every placed tile: beside it, above it, below it, lined up with either corner, standing up or lying down. Of all the spots where it overlaps nothing, it takes the one whose farthest corner is closest to the starting point. That keeps the cluster growing like a round crystal and pushes every tile into the tightest nook available. Ties go to the spot that touches the most existing edge.

The density trace shows how much of the smallest disc around the cluster is covered. Like the circle chain, it wobbles: it drops whenever a big tile sticks out, and climbs when smaller ones fill in behind it.

The primes are the mortar

Every composite tile is at least 2 wide. A crack only 1 unit wide can only ever be filled by a prime's 1×p strip, because the primes are the only numbers whose one and only rectangle is a single unit wide. They keep arriving forever, ever longer, at irregular moments: the same irregularity that the Riemann Hypothesis is about.

Switch to prime × prime only to see the opposite: only rectangles with two prime sides (2×2, 2×3, 3×3, 2×5, …). Every tile is then at least 2 wide, the 1-wide cracks stay open forever, and the cluster covers noticeably less of its disc.

Composites build the walls; the primes are the thin strips that seal them.

Perfect, no gaps

Packing tightly always leaves holes. The Perfect mode follows a different rule, in the spirit of Henle and Henle's tiling of the plane by one square of every size: the tiled area is a complete rectangle at every step.

Take the smallest tile not yet used and lay it along one side of the rectangle. Fill the rest of that side with one or two unused tiles of exactly the right size, so the result is again a perfect rectangle. Then move on to the next side: right, top, left, bottom. Fitting fillers always exist, because only finitely many tiles have been used and there are infinitely many composite rectangles of every width from 2 up, plus unused prime strips wherever a width of 1 is needed.

So every tile eventually gets its turn, no tile is ever used twice, and the rectangle grows in every direction without a single gap. Carried on forever, this tiles the whole plane, with every prime as one 1×p strip and every rectangle of every other number exactly once. The covered area always equals the rectangle.

The price is order. Fillers are pulled forward out of turn, so the largest number used runs further and further ahead of the smallest one still waiting (the trace under the tiling, on a log scale). The Henles noticed the same extravagance in their square tiling. Unlike a Penrose tiling, which reuses two shapes by local rules, this one uses every shape exactly once, by a global rule.

Whether the prime×prime rectangles, one of each, could tile the whole plane with a cleverer rule than this greedy one, I do not know of a result. For squares the question is settled: one square of every whole-number side tiles the plane (Henle and Henle, 2008), but squares with prime sides cannot (Henle and Henle, 2015).
