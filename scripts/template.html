The Riemann Hypothesis, drawn with circles

The most famous problem in mathematics, explained with a compass, a ruler and one idea about rotation. No formulas needed until the very end.

By Sanjin Redzic · Interactive: every figure responds to the controls

STEP 1 · THE CIRCLES

One circle for every prime

Prime numbers are the numbers you can only divide by 1 and themselves: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, … Start at 3. Draw a circle of radius 3 standing on a line. Next to it, a circle of radius 5, placed so the two just touch. Then 7, then 11, and so on: one circle for every prime, each touching the one before.

Mark the centre of every circle. The primes jump around unpredictably, yet the centres line up surprisingly well along a smooth curve (dashed). That curve comes from the prime number theorem from 1896, which tells us how many primes there are on average. The dots never sit exactly on it, though. That tiny mismatch is what the whole story is about.

STEP 2 · COMPLEX NUMBERS ARE NOT COMPLEX

The imaginary direction is just sideways

To describe a centre we need two numbers: how far along the line it is, and how high up. Mathematicians write this as one number, x + i·y, and call it a complex number. The name is unlucky. Nothing is complex or imaginary about it.

Think of i as an instruction: turn a quarter of a circle. Multiply by i once and an arrow pointing right now points up. Twice, and it points left, which is why i·i = −1. The "imaginary" axis is simply the direction at right angles to the ordinary number line. It was there all along; we just usually look along the line and don't see it.

So every circle centre is one complex number: the real part says how far along, the imaginary part is the prime itself, the height. Seen from the origin, each centre also has an angle. That angle will turn out to carry the whole secret.

STEP 3 · THE WOBBLE

The centres wobble around the curve

Measure how far each centre lies from the smooth curve. After a cluster of primes that are close together, the chain gets ahead of the curve. After a big gap between primes, it falls behind. Most of the time it sits a little behind; that slight lean is itself a known effect of the primes. The first time the chain catches up and crosses the curve is at the circles 109 and 113, right before the big gap to 127.

The wobble grows as the circles grow. Scale it by the size the theory expects, and something remarkable appears: for every prime up to 100 million, the wobble stays in the same band, swinging up and down in an irregular but strangely rhythmic way.

STEP 4 · THE WOBBLE IS MUSIC

Hidden notes inside the wobble

A chord on a piano is several notes at once. You can split any wobbly signal into its notes too, a bit like an ear does. Do that with the wobble of the circles, and clear notes ring out at 14.13, 21.02, 25.01, 30.42, 32.94, …

Dashed lines: the zeros of the Riemann zeta function. Same circles, same method; the random numbers have the same density as the primes.

Those numbers are famous. They are the zeros of the Riemann zeta function, found by Bernhard Riemann in 1859. Swap the primes for random numbers of the same density and the notes vanish. The music belongs to the primes.

Each note behaves like a wheel turning at its own speed. Put wheels on wheels, one per note, and follow the tip: it reproduces the wobble. The more wheels, the closer the match.

STEP 5 · WHAT THE HYPOTHESIS SAYS

All wheels stay the same size

Riemann noticed that his zeros all seemed to lie on one straight line in the plane of complex numbers, the critical line. His hypothesis is that every zero lies there, infinitely many of them.

In the circle picture this has a simple meaning. A zero on the line is a wheel that keeps its size. A zero off the line would be a wheel that keeps growing. Try it: move the first zero off the line and watch what happens to the wobble far out.

Wobble built from the first 138 zeros, out to primes of size 10⁴⁰. Real part ½ means on the critical line.

The Riemann Hypothesis, in circle language: the wobble of the prime circles never grows beyond the size you see here, no matter how far you go. The primes are as regular as they can possibly be.

all zeros on Re(s) = ½ ⟺ wobble stays within about y1.5 for every prime radius y

This equivalence is a classical theorem by Helge von Koch (1901), written here in terms of the circles.

STEP 6 · WHERE IT STANDS

Checked by computers, proved this year?

For more than 160 years nobody could prove it, although computers checked trillions of zeros and found every one of them on the line. In November 2026, mathematician Levent Alpöge announced a proof developed together with an AI model, written in the proof language Lean. The Lean and Mathlib communities certified it the next day. The written-out version runs past 250,000 pages, and mathematicians are now working to understand it. Some groups still dispute whether a proof nobody can read counts.

If it stands, the picture above is now a fact about all primes, forever: the circles never stray further from their curve than the wheels allow.

It would also settle far more than one question. For over a century, mathematicians have proved hundreds of results that begin with the words “assuming the Riemann Hypothesis is true”: about how primes are spread out, how fast we can test whether a number is prime, and much more. Alpöge's proof also claims the generalized version of the hypothesis. If it holds, all of those results stop being conditional and become theorems at once. Mankind would have the proof, and everything built on it, before anyone fully understands why it works.

Why I made this

Just as the mathematician behind the new proof worked with an AI model, I used Claude to write the code and run the computations for this page. My own small contribution is the circles, and the wish behind them: I want more people to understand this beautiful problem. Riemann's original framing, with the zeta function and its analytic continuation, can look forbidding. The beauty underneath it is not.

Complex numbers are one of the main building blocks of mathematics, and they are not mysterious: they are the ordinary numbers plus one quarter-turn. With that single idea, a compass and a row of prime circles, you can see what the most famous problem in mathematics is really about.
