The zeros on a circle, and a tangent line

Every zero of the zeta function can be moved onto one circle. The critical line becomes the unit circle, and the Riemann Hypothesis becomes a sentence about 0 and 1: every zero is exactly as far from 0 as it is from 1. Then send the circle to its tangent line, lean the line, and see why such a simple sentence is so hard to prove.

1. One circle for all the zeros

Send every zero ρ to the point w = 1 − 1/ρ. The critical line Re ρ = ½ lands exactly on the unit circle. A zero to the right of the line would land inside, one to the left outside. Each point on the circle is then sent to the tangent line at w = 1 along a straight ray from the opposite point −1.

Why the circle? |w| = 1 means |ρ − 1| = |ρ|: the zero is as far from 1 as from 0. The points equally far from 0 and 1 form the perpendicular bisector of the segment between them, and that is the line Re s = ½.

RH ⟺ |ρ − 1| = |ρ| for every zero ρ ⟺ every w = 1 − 1/ρ lies on the unit circle

On the tangent line the zeros land at heights of exactly ±1/γ: 0.0707 for the first zero, 0.0476 for the second, 0.0400 for the third, and so on, crowding into the touching point. In general the landing point is 1 − 1/(ρ − ½), which is on the tangent line exactly when ρ − ½ is purely imaginary.

2. Leaning the tangent line

Each landing point has two parts: a height along the line (1/γ) and a sideways offset (zero on the critical line, about (β − ½)/γ² off it). Lean the line by an angle φ and you read a mix of the two: height·cos φ + offset·sin φ. At 90° you look along the line, edge-on, like looking at the sphere from above on the complex numbers page.

Edge-on, every zero on the critical line collapses into the single touching point, and the Riemann Hypothesis becomes: nothing sticks out of one point. But a fixed lean only rotates the view. It never makes an off-line zero bigger, and its offset (β − ½)/γ² fades for high zeros.

3. The tangency zone, and Li's magnifier

Near the touching point the circle and its tangent line are only about 1/(2γ²) apart, and an off-line zero's distance from the circle shrinks the same way, about (β − ½)/γ². The higher a zero, the deeper it hides in the touching point. Xian-Jin Li found the way to magnify it: raise every w to the n-th power. On the circle wⁿ only turns. Off the circle it spirals.

Li's criterion adds up 1 − wⁿ over all zeros, a number λₙ. If every w is on the circle, each conjugate pair gives 2 − 2cos(nθ), never negative, so λₙ ≥ 0. Li proved the converse: RH holds exactly when λₙ ≥ 0 for every n. The first one is λ₁ = 1 + γ_E/2 − ½ ln 4π ≈ 0.0231 (γ_E is Euler's constant). Every λₙ computed so far is positive. The catch is visible above: a zero at height γ only starts to show at n ≈ γ²/(β − ½), so a hypothetical off-line zero at height 10¹² would hide until n ≈ 10²⁴. That is why the criterion must say every n.

All the action happens at the touching point, where circle and line meet. That is the Riemann Hypothesis in miniature: an infinitely detailed question squeezed into the place where the gap goes to zero.
