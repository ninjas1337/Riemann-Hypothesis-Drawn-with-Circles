The factorisation line, primes left empty

Lay every rectangle of every factorisation on the real line, one after another, from the smallest up: 2×2, 2×3, 2×4, 3×3, 2×5, 2×6, 3×4, … Each prime has no rectangle with both sides at least 2, so it leaves an empty gap of its own length. Watch the line develop.

Colour = the short side: 2 3 4 or 5 6 and up prime, left empty

The whole line so far, squeezed sideways (heights not to scale). Orange ticks under the line mark the empty prime gaps.

How much of the line is empty?

Measured with the rectangles lying down, so every number n adds length in proportion to its size.

With the rectangles lying down, each composite rectangle a×b adds its long side b to the line, and each prime p adds an empty stretch of length p. Up to N the rectangles add up to about (π²/6 − 1)·N²/2, because there are about N/a rectangles with short side a, with lengths up to N/a. The empty stretches add up to the sum of all primes up to N, which the prime number theorem puts at about li(N²) ≈ N²/(2 ln N).

empty share ≈ li(N²) / (li(N²) + composite length) ≈ 1 / (1 + (π²/6 − 1)·ln N)

The dashed prediction uses Riemann's refinement for the sum of primes, li(N²) − ½·li(N3/2) − ⅓·li(N4/3) − …, which accounts for the squares and higher powers of primes. What is left between the two curves is the wobble that comes from the zeta zeros.

So the primes' emptiness thins out, but only like 1/ln N: about 20% of the line is empty at N = 1,000, 15% at 10,000 and 12% at 100,000. The famous π²/6 = 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + … turns up because of the squares 1/a² in that count.

The composites fill the line almost evenly; the primes are the holes, and the holes get rarer exactly as fast as the primes do. The small wobble of the measured share around the prediction is the same prime wobble the circle chain shows.
