Two clockworks: Fourier and zeta

Turning circles on turning circles. On the left, an ordinary Fourier series: every circle turns at a whole multiple of the first, so the picture repeats. On the right, the zeta zeros: every circle has its own permanent angle and its own speed, and the picture never repeats. Make the left circles wobble, or move a zero off the critical line so its circle starts to breathe, and see why only one of those would break the Riemann Hypothesis.

What to look for

Permanent angles. On the left every arm is an exact sine. On the right each zero ρ = ½ + iγ is divided by ρ + 1 on its way into the wobble, which turns its arm by a fixed angle: 83.9° for the first zero, creeping towards 90° for higher ones. The arms are almost sines, each a little off.

No repeat. The left trace is periodic. The right trace never repeats, because the zeta frequencies are not whole multiples of each other. It is widely believed, though not proved, that they are completely independent (the linear independence hypothesis). The alignment meter shows how closely the arms point the same way: on the left it returns to the same values every period; on the right it wanders, and now and then the arms bunch together. Kronecker's theorem says they come arbitrarily close to full alignment if you wait long enough, which is how Littlewood proved in 1914 that π(x) − li(x) changes sign infinitely often.

Breathing circles. On the critical line every circle keeps its size (after scaling by y1.5/ln y). Pick a zero and move it to β > ½: its circle grows like yβ−½, the trace swells, and that one wheel eventually takes over. The Riemann Hypothesis says no circle in this clockwork ever breathes.

Wobbling versus breathing

Circles can change size in two very different ways, and the two panels show one each.

A wobbling circle is three steady circles in disguise. Turn up the wobble on the left. A circle whose size pulses periodically, r(1 + w·sin Ωt), drawing sin(mt), is exactly the same as three circles of fixed size:

r(1 + w sin Ωt)·sin(mt) = r sin(mt) + (rw/2)·cos((m − Ω)t) − (rw/2)·cos((m + Ω)t)

This is the product-to-sum identity, and it is how AM radio works: the wobble only adds two new frequencies (sidebands) next to the old one. Nothing grows. The clockwork just gets more wheels.

A breathing circle has a complex speed. A circle that grows steadily, r·eσt, cannot be rebuilt from steady circles at all. It is the imaginary part of e(σ + im)t: the turning speed itself has picked up a real part. That is exactly what a zeta zero ρ = β + iγ is. Its wheel is yρ = yβ·eiγ·ln y: γ sets the speed, β sets the growth. After the common y½ is scaled away, β − ½ is the breathing rate of that circle.

So the Riemann Hypothesis can be read as a statement about clockworks: the music of the primes may wobble as much as it likes, but none of its circles breathes. Every zero found so far, more than 12 trillion of them, has β = ½ exactly.
